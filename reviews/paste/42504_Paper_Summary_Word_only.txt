This paper analyzes Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) in a population-level setting designed to match common implementations: the actor is a fixed-covariance squashed Gaussian (linear mean in pre-tanh space, then tanh to bounded actions); each outer iteration takes only one projected reverse-KL gradient step toward a moving target policy; and the learner has a critic oracle, meaning the estimated Q-function at iteration n differs from the true soft Q-function under the current actor by at most a uniform error epsilon-crit across all states and actions. The authors compare two ways to define the target policy in entropy-regularized policy improvement. The first is the classical Gibbs target in Equation 4: the next target policy is proportional to exp(minus Q-hat-n divided by temperature tau). The second is a policy mirror descent target in Equation 5 with proximal step size lambda; the sequence of MD targets is updated from the previous target policy, not from the current actor policy.

Assuming concentrability of the optimal occupancy measure and a curvature condition on the critic based on a Legendre-type operator (half the curvature constant R-Q-hat must be smaller than tau; Theorem 2), the reverse-KL actor objective becomes strongly convex and smooth on a parameter ball of radius R. The theory separates two effects: target drift (how quickly the target moves when the critic estimate and data distribution change) and actor tracking error (how far one KL gradient step falls short of the moving target). For mirror-descent targets, Lemma 9 shows the ideal actor parameters shift by an amount on the order of lambda between iterations, while Theorem 1 also includes terms scaling like one-over-lambda that reflect how fast the MD chain improves the target. Corollary 1 highlights the Gibbs case: even if replay data change slowly, the tracking bound still contains a term that does not vanish with training and scales like one-over-tau, so the actor cannot track a fresh Gibbs softmax target arbitrarily closely when it only takes partial updates. For MD targets, lambda is an explicit dial: smaller lambda slows target motion (easier tracking) but also slows improvement along the MD chain.

The main finite-time result is Theorem 3, a best-iterate guarantee on the regularized value gap after N actor updates: the gap is on the order of N raised to the power negative one-fifth, plus approximation error, when lambda is chosen on the order of N raised to the power negative four-fifths and the actor step size h is small enough relative to tau and R-Q-hat. The authors present this as a population-level guarantee for partial KL steps with a squashed Gaussian actor and a quantitative Gibbs-versus-MD comparison for tracking error. Experiments appear only in Appendix K: a small tabular MDP where critics can be solved exactly, with perturbations that vary critic curvature while keeping the same epsilon-crit (Figure 1), and plots of MD versus Gibbs tracking error as a function of lambda (Figure 2). There is no empirical check of the negative-one-fifth rate on deep continuous-control benchmarks.
